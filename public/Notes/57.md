# **Barlovic, R., Santen, L., Schadschneider, A., & Schreckenberg, M. (1998). Metastable states in cellular automata for traffic flow. arXiv:cond-mat/9804170.**

1. Main idea: How can cellular automata reproduce metastable traffic states and hysteresis observed in real traffic? The traditional Nagel–Schreckenberg (NaSch) model can simulate spontaneous traffic jams but does not reproduce the long-lived metastable states observed in real traffic. The authors investigate how modifying vehicle acceleration behavior can generate these phenomena.

   The key hypothesis is that **vehicles restarting from a complete stop accelerate more slowly than vehicles already moving**. This reduces the outflow from traffic jams and allows free-flowing and congested traffic states to coexist at the same density.

2. The authors investigate three slow-to-start (S2S) cellular automaton models.

   | Model | Modification | Main characteristic |
   | ----- | ------------ | ------------------- |
   | T2 model | Stopped vehicles have a reduced acceleration probability when exactly one empty cell is ahead | Spatial slow-to-start rule |
   | BJH model | Vehicles previously forced to brake have a reduced probability of restarting | Temporal rule requiring memory |
   | VDR model | Random braking probability depends on the vehicle's previous velocity | Velocity-dependent randomization |

   All three models are modifications of the NaSch model.

3. Proposed model: Velocity-Dependent Randomization (VDR).

   1. The model retains the four basic NaSch update steps:
      1. Acceleration
      2. Braking to avoid collisions
      3. Randomization
      4. Vehicle movement

   2. Unlike the original NaSch model, the random braking probability depends on the vehicle's velocity at the previous time step:

      \[
      p(v)=
      \begin{cases}
      p_0, & v=0\\
      p, & v>0
      \end{cases}
      \]

   3. The model uses:
      1. \(p_0\): Randomization probability for stopped vehicles.
      2. \(p\): Randomization probability for moving vehicles.
      3. \(p_0>p\): Stopped vehicles are less likely to restart immediately.

   4. When \(p_0=p\), the model reduces to the original NaSch model.

   5. Main simulation parameters:
      1. Maximum speed: \(v_{\max}=5\) cells/time step.
      2. Moving-vehicle randomization: \(p=1/64\approx0.015625\).
      3. Stopped-vehicle randomization: \(p_0=0.75\).
      4. Road length: \(L=10000\) cells.
      5. Periodic boundary conditions.

   **Key difference:** The VDR model introduces slow-to-start behavior without requiring additional vehicle memory or longer-range interactions.

4. Two initial traffic configurations are used to investigate metastability.

   1. Homogeneous initial condition:
      1. Vehicles are distributed approximately evenly along the road.
      2. Interactions between vehicles are initially minimized.
      3. This configuration produces the upper, high-flow branch of the fundamental diagram.

   2. Megajam initial condition:
      1. Vehicles are initially concentrated into one large stationary cluster.
      2. The system evolves into a state containing a large jam and a free-flowing region.
      3. This configuration produces the lower, low-flow branch.

   **Key result:** At certain densities, the average traffic flow depends on the initial vehicle configuration, rather than density alone.

5. Results: Hysteresis in the fundamental diagram.

   Figure 4 shows the flow-density relationship for the VDR model.

   1. The authors identify two traffic states at the same density:
      1. High-flow state: Vehicles remain approximately homogeneously distributed.
      2. Low-flow state: Vehicles form a large traffic jam separated from a free-flowing region.

   2. Between densities \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\), both states can persist for long periods.

   3. Increasing density gradually from a free-flowing state produces the upper branch.

   4. Decreasing density gradually from a congested state produces the lower branch.

   5. As system size increases, the density interval between \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) becomes narrower.

   **Key result:** Traffic flow is history-dependent. The same vehicle density can produce either high throughput or severe congestion.

6. Results: Reduced jam outflow causes phase separation.

   1. In the original NaSch model, traffic jams tend to form multiple clusters with an approximately exponential size distribution.

   2. In the VDR model, traffic can separate into:
      1. One large, dense traffic jam.
      2. One low-density, free-flowing region.

   3. The mechanism is:
      1. Vehicles inside a jam have a high probability of remaining stopped.
      2. Vehicles leave the jam more slowly.
      3. The reduced outflow lowers the density downstream.
      4. The downstream region becomes sufficiently sparse for vehicles to travel freely.
      5. A large jam can therefore coexist with freely moving traffic.

   4. The authors derive approximate flow equations:

      Homogeneous free-flow branch:

      \[
      J_{\mathrm{hom}}(\rho)=\rho(v_{\max}-p)
      \]

      Phase-separated congested branch:

      \[
      J_{\mathrm{sep}}(\rho)=(1-p_0)(1-\rho)
      \]

      For \(p_0=0.75\):

      \[
      J_{\mathrm{sep}}(\rho)=0.25(1-\rho)
      \]

   These approximations are most applicable when \(p_0\gg p\) and \(v_{\max}>1\).

   **Key result:** Reduced outflow from traffic jams, also known as a capacity drop, is the central mechanism responsible for metastability and phase separation.

7. Results: Stability and spontaneous breakdown of free-flow traffic.

   1. Figure 5 investigates the evolution of traffic jam length at density \(\rho=0.095\).
      1. Individual traffic jams exhibit large fluctuations in length.
      2. The average jam length decays exponentially over 10000 samples.
      3. Once the jam disappears, the system reaches a stable homogeneous state.

   2. Figure 6 shows the spontaneous formation of a traffic jam using:
      1. Density: \(\rho=0.15\).
      2. Road length: \(L=400\).
      3. Moving-vehicle randomization: \(p=0.01\).
      4. Stopped-vehicle randomization: \(p_0=0.5\).

   3. The initially homogeneous traffic state survives for approximately **93000 simulation steps** before a jam forms spontaneously.

   4. The authors find that:
      1. Homogeneous high-flow states can have extremely long lifetimes.
      2. Random velocity fluctuations can eventually trigger congestion.
      3. Once congestion forms, reduced jam outflow stabilizes the congested state.

   **Key result:** A traffic system may operate at high flow for a long time before suddenly breaking down, even without any external bottleneck or accident.

8. Results: Comparison between slow-to-start models.

   1. T2 model:
      1. Simulated with \(v_{\max}=5\), \(p=0.01\), and \(p_t=0.75\).
      2. Produces hysteresis and metastable traffic states.
      3. The fundamental diagram develops an inflection point at high density.
      4. For \(v_{\max}=1\) and \(p_t=1\), completely blocked states occur at densities \(\rho\geq0.5\).

   2. BJH model:
      1. Simulated with \(v_{\max}=5\), \(p=0.01\), and \(p_s=0.75\).
      2. Also exhibits metastability and hysteresis.
      3. Unlike the T2 model, its fundamental diagram remains convex.
      4. The slow-to-start rule requires remembering whether a vehicle previously had to brake.

   3. VDR model:
      1. Produces similar metastability and hysteresis for \(v_{\max}>1\).
      2. Does not require additional memory or extended interactions.
      3. Provides a simpler way to incorporate slow-to-start behavior into NaSch.

   **Key result:** The occurrence of metastable states is not tied to a particular slow-to-start mechanism. Different rules can produce similar macroscopic traffic phenomena.

9. Main conclusions.

   1. **Metastable traffic states:** A high-flow state can persist even at densities where traffic jams are possible.

   2. **Hysteresis:** Traffic flow depends on the system's history and initial conditions, not only on vehicle density.

   3. **Capacity drop:** Reduced vehicle discharge from traffic jams is essential for producing phase-separated traffic states.

   4. **Spontaneous congestion:** Random fluctuations can trigger sudden traffic breakdown after long periods of apparently stable flow.

   5. **Model simplicity:** Realistic macroscopic phenomena can be reproduced using a relatively simple modification of the NaSch model.

   6. **Traffic management:** Maintaining homogeneous high-flow states may help maximize road throughput. The authors discuss controlling vehicle inflow as a possible practical application.

   **Main contribution:** The paper demonstrates that introducing velocity-dependent slow-to-start behavior into the NaSch cellular automaton can reproduce metastability, hysteresis, capacity drop, and spontaneous traffic breakdown.

   Paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9804170

# **Feng, T., Liu, K., & Liang, C. (2023). An improved cellular automata traffic flow model considering driving styles. Sustainability, 15(2), 952.**

1. Main idea: How can different driving styles be incorporated into cellular automata (CA) traffic models to better reproduce real traffic flow and congestion? Traditional CA models, such as the Nagel–Schreckenberg (NaSch) model, generally assume homogeneous driver behavior and do not sufficiently represent differences in acceleration, deceleration, and car-following behavior.

   The authors propose an improved CA model incorporating three driving styles (aggressive, moderate, and calm) and two mechanisms (speed adaptation and over-acceleration) to reproduce traffic congestion formation, dissipation, and transitions among traffic phases.

2. Driving style classification using real vehicle trajectory data.

   1. The authors use the Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) dataset:
      1. Location: US-101, Los Angeles, California.
      2. Observation period: 7:50–8:05 a.m.
      3. Sampling frequency: 10 Hz.
      4. Extracted data: 1104 car-following datasets containing 653,568 vehicle trajectory samples.

   2. Ten driving behavior indicators are selected, including:
      1. Average and maximum velocity.
      2. Average acceleration and deceleration.
      3. Space headway and time headway.
      4. Speed differences and fluctuations.

   3. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is applied to reduce the dimensionality of the driving behavior indicators.

   4. K-means clustering is used to classify drivers into three categories.

   5. Classification results:
      1. Aggressive drivers: **24%**
      2. Moderate drivers: **54%**
      3. Calm drivers: **22%**

   | Driving style | Maximum velocity (km/h) | Average acceleration (m/s²) | Average deceleration (m/s²) |
   | ------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
   | Aggressive    | 73.21                   | 4.32                        | 4.74                        |
   | Moderate      | 65.18                   | 2.62                        | 2.43                        |
   | Calm          | 60.86                   | 1.58                        | 1.33                        |

   **Key result:** Aggressive drivers tend to maintain shorter distances, drive faster, and accelerate or decelerate more strongly. Calm drivers maintain larger following distances and exhibit smoother driving behavior.

3. Proposed improved CA model.

   The model extends the NaSch and Kerner–Klenov–Wolf (KKW) models by introducing two mechanisms.

   1. Speed adaptation:
      1. When the preceding vehicle is within a synchronization distance, the following vehicle adjusts its velocity according to the preceding vehicle.
      2. If the preceding vehicle is faster, the following vehicle accelerates.
      3. If the preceding vehicle is slower, the following vehicle decelerates.
      4. If both vehicles have the same velocity, the following vehicle maintains its velocity.

      The synchronization distance is defined as:

      \[
      D_n=d+kv_nt
      \]

      where:
      - \(D_n\): Synchronization distance.
      - \(d\): Constant distance parameter.
      - \(k\): Synchronization coefficient.
      - \(v_n\): Current vehicle velocity.
      - \(t\): Time step.

   2. Over-acceleration:
      1. A following vehicle may accelerate even when it is already traveling at least as fast as the preceding vehicle.
      2. Over-acceleration occurs probabilistically when the vehicle is within the synchronization distance.
      3. This mechanism introduces speed fluctuations and helps reproduce transitions between traffic phases.

   3. Different driving styles are assigned different vehicle parameters:

      | Driving style | Maximum speed (cells/s) | Acceleration (cells/s²) | Deceleration (cells/s²) |
      | ------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
      | Aggressive    | 19                     | 4                       | 4                       |
      | Moderate      | 18                     | 2                       | 2                       |
      | Calm          | 16                     | 1                       | 1                       |

   **Key result:** Combining speed adaptation, over-acceleration, and heterogeneous driver parameters allows the model to reproduce more complex traffic behavior than the traditional NaSch model.

4. Simulation setup.

   1. Road configuration: Single-lane circular road with periodic boundary conditions.
   2. Road length: 2000 cells.
   3. Cell length: 1 m.
   4. Vehicle length: 7 cells.
   5. Simulation duration: 2000 s.
   6. Time step: 1 s.
   7. Driving style proportions:
      1. Aggressive: 20%
      2. Moderate: 60%
      3. Calm: 20%

   Main model parameters:

   | Parameter | Value |
   | --------- | ----- |
   | Synchronization coefficient \(k\) | 2 |
   | Distance constant \(d\) | 0 |
   | Over-acceleration probability \(p_{a1}\) | 0.3 |
   | Over-acceleration probability \(p_{a2}\) | 0.2 |
   | Slow-start probability \(p_{sts}\) | 0.3 |
   | Random slowing probability \(p_{slow}\) | 0.2 |
   | Velocity threshold \(v_p\) | 9 cells/s |

5. Results: Comparison with the NaSch model.

   Figure 5 presents the flow-density relationship for both models.

   1. NaSch model:
      1. Maximum flow: **0.751**
      2. Optimal density: **0.165**
      3. Flow increases with density until reaching its maximum, then decreases as congestion develops.

   2. Improved CA model:
      1. Maximum flow: **0.896**
      2. Optimal density: **0.133**
      3. Maximum flow is approximately **19.3% higher** than the reported NaSch maximum (the paper's conclusion describes the improvement as approximately 15%).
      4. After reaching the optimal density, traffic flow experiences a sudden decrease.

   | Model | Maximum flow | Optimal density |
   | ----- | ------------ | --------------- |
   | NaSch | 0.751        | 0.165           |
   | Improved CA | 0.896 | 0.133 |

   3. When density is between 0.143 and 0.805, the NaSch model generally produces slightly higher flow than the improved model.

   4. This difference occurs because speed adaptation causes vehicles to maintain safer following distances and adjust their speed according to preceding vehicles.

   **Key result:** The improved model achieves a higher maximum traffic flow but can produce lower flow at intermediate and high densities because vehicles adapt their speed to maintain safe distances.

6. Results: Reproduction of three traffic phases.

   The improved CA model reproduces the three traffic phases described by Kerner's three-phase traffic theory.

   1. Free flow (F):
      1. Vehicles move at relatively high speeds.
      2. Interactions between vehicles are limited.
      3. Flow increases approximately linearly with density.

   2. Synchronized flow (S):
      1. Vehicles adapt their speed to preceding vehicles.
      2. Vehicle speeds become correlated.
      3. Traffic flow decreases without immediately forming severe congestion.

   3. Wide moving jam (J):
      1. Vehicle density becomes high.
      2. Speeds decrease significantly.
      3. Traffic jams propagate backward through the road.

   4. The authors identify the following transitions:
      1. **F→S:** Free flow to synchronized flow.
      2. **S→J:** Synchronized flow to wide moving jam.
      3. **S→F:** Synchronized flow returning to free flow.

   5. According to the fundamental diagram:
      1. Around density 0.133, the improved model reaches its maximum flow.
      2. Between approximately 0.103 and 0.26, the model can exhibit the F→S transition.
      3. Above density 0.26, the S→J transition occurs.

   **Key result:** Unlike the basic NaSch model, the improved CA model can reproduce transitions among free flow, synchronized flow, and wide moving jams.

7. Results: Influence of driving styles on traffic flow.

   Figure 6 compares the flow-density and velocity-density relationships for different driving styles.

   1. Aggressive drivers:
      1. Have higher maximum speeds and acceleration.
      2. Can produce higher traffic flow and road capacity.
      3. Leave the free-flow phase earlier as density increases.
      4. Produce stronger speed fluctuations and lower traffic stability.

   2. Calm drivers:
      1. Maintain safer distances from preceding vehicles.
      2. Accelerate and decelerate more gently.
      3. Produce lower maximum traffic flow.
      4. Maintain traffic stability for longer periods.

   3. Moderate drivers:
      1. Exhibit intermediate acceleration and speed characteristics.
      2. Produce traffic behavior between aggressive and calm driving styles.

   4. Authors find:
      1. Aggressive driving can increase traffic capacity to approximately **181% of the calm-driving capacity**.
      2. Differences between driving styles are especially noticeable at densities of 0.165–0.26.
      3. When density exceeds 0.441, all driving styles converge toward the wide moving jam phase.

   **Key result:** Aggressive driving improves traffic throughput but reduces stability, while calm driving produces smoother and more stable traffic flow at the cost of lower capacity.

8. Results: Traffic congestion formation and dissipation.

   Figures 7 and 8 compare the traffic evolution of the NaSch model and the improved CA model.

   1. The NaSch model:
      1. Produces large congestion regions.
      2. Traffic jams propagate upstream over time.
      3. Vehicle trajectories show extensive congestion bands.

   2. The improved CA model:
      1. Produces fewer congested regions.
      2. Traffic jams dissipate more effectively.
      3. Speed adaptation allows vehicles to maintain more uniform speeds.
      4. Congestion regions decrease gradually over time.

   3. At density \(\rho=0.42\), the vehicle trajectory diagrams show that the improved CA model produces fewer blocking strips than the NaSch model.

   **Key result:** The improved CA model can reduce traffic congestion and improve traffic stability through speed adaptation, even when the overall average vehicle speed is not particularly high.

9. Main conclusions.

   1. **Driving heterogeneity:** Different driving styles significantly affect traffic flow, congestion development, and vehicle interactions.

   2. **Traffic capacity improvement:** The improved CA model produces a higher maximum traffic flow than the traditional NaSch model.

   3. **Three-phase traffic behavior:** The model reproduces free flow, synchronized flow, wide moving jams, and transitions among these states.

   4. **Driving style trade-off:** Aggressive drivers increase road throughput but reduce traffic stability, while calm drivers improve stability at the expense of capacity.

   5. **Congestion dissipation:** Speed adaptation and over-acceleration help reduce large congestion regions and support more stable traffic movement.

   6. **Limitations:** The model is restricted to single-lane traffic with periodic boundary conditions. It does not explicitly simulate lane-changing behavior, weather effects, or general multilane mixed traffic.

   7. **Future work:** The authors propose extending the model to multilane highways and carrying out more detailed comparisons with real-world traffic data.

   **Main contribution:** The paper demonstrates that incorporating empirically classified driving styles and speed adaptation mechanisms into a cellular automaton model can improve the representation of traffic heterogeneity, reproduce three-phase traffic behavior, and reduce simulated congestion compared with the traditional NaSch model.

# **Jiang, Y., Wang, S., Yao, Z., Zhao, B., & Wang, Y. (2021). A cellular automata model for mixed traffic flow considering the driving behavior of connected automated vehicle platoons. Physica A: Statistical Mechanics and its Applications, 582, 126262.**

1. Main idea: How does the introduction of connected automated vehicles (CAVs) and their platooning behavior affect traffic flow, road capacity, congestion, and speed stability?

   Traditional cellular automata (CA) models mainly focus on human-driven vehicles (HDVs) or individual automated vehicles. However, these models generally do not account for the formation of CAV platoons through vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication.

   The authors propose an improved CA model that considers the different car-following behaviors of HDVs and CAVs, including the ability of CAVs to form platoons and move synchronously.

2. Three car-following modes are introduced.

   Figure 1 illustrates the three car-following modes in mixed traffic.

   1. Human-Driven Vehicle (HDV) mode:
      1. The following vehicle is human-driven.
      2. The leading vehicle can be either an HDV or a CAV.
      3. Human drivers require time to perceive and react to the preceding vehicle.
      4. Random deceleration is introduced to reproduce human driving uncertainty.

   2. Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC) mode:
      1. The following vehicle is a CAV.
      2. The leading vehicle is an HDV.
      3. The CAV detects the preceding vehicle using onboard sensors.
      4. Its reaction time is shorter than that of human drivers.

   3. Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) mode:
      1. Both the following and leading vehicles are CAVs.
      2. Vehicles exchange information through V2V communication.
      3. Vehicles can synchronize acceleration and deceleration.
      4. The reaction delay is assumed to be approximately zero.
      5. Vehicles maintain a small constant spacing and form platoons.

   | Following vehicle | Leading vehicle | Car-following mode |
   | ----------------- | --------------- | ------------------ |
   | HDV               | HDV             | HDV                |
   | HDV               | CAV             | HDV                |
   | CAV               | HDV             | ACC                |
   | CAV               | CAV             | CACC               |

   **Key result:** CACC allows consecutive CAVs to maintain smaller distances and move synchronously, potentially increasing traffic capacity.

3. Proposed cellular automata model.

   The model modifies traditional CA rules by considering the safety distance, vehicle reaction time, and platoon behavior.

   1. Safety distance:

      The safety distance depends on the car-following mode.

      For HDV and ACC modes:

      \[
      d_{\mathrm{safe}}=v_n\tau+\frac{v_n^2-v_{n-1}^2}{2B}
      \]

      where:
      - \(v_n\): Speed of the following vehicle.
      - \(v_{n-1}\): Speed of the leading vehicle.
      - \(\tau\): Reaction time.
      - \(B\): Maximum deceleration.

      The reaction time depends on vehicle type:

      \[
      \tau=
      \begin{cases}
      \tau_{\mathrm{HDV}}, & \text{HDV mode}\\
      \tau_{\mathrm{CAV}}, & \text{ACC mode}
      \end{cases}
      \]

      For CACC mode, the safety distance is constant:

      \[
      d_{\mathrm{safe}}=d_s^{\mathrm{CACC}}
      \]

   2. Acceleration:
      1. HDVs and ACC vehicles accelerate when the distance to the preceding vehicle exceeds the safety distance.
      2. CACC vehicles adjust their speed according to the future speed of the preceding CAV.
      3. This allows vehicles to synchronize their movement within a platoon.

   3. Deceleration:
      1. Vehicles decelerate when the available distance is smaller than or equal to the safety distance.
      2. In CACC mode, vehicles follow the speed changes of the leading CAV.
      3. Vehicles within a platoon can accelerate and brake synchronously.

   4. Randomization:
      1. Only HDVs experience random slowing.
      2. Random deceleration occurs with probability \(p_{\mathrm{slow}}\).
      3. The random slowing state is maintained during the driver's reaction-time interval.
      4. ACC and CACC vehicles do not experience random slowing in the model.

   **Key result:** By incorporating shorter reaction times, synchronized vehicle movement, and different safety distances, the model can represent mixed traffic containing both human-driven vehicles and CAV platoons.

4. Simulation setup.

   1. Road configuration: Single-lane freeway with periodic boundary conditions.
   2. Road length: 400 m.
   3. Cell length: 0.01 m.
   4. Vehicle length: 5 m.
   5. Simulation time step: 0.1 s.
   6. Total simulation steps: 2000.
   7. Measurements are collected during the final 1000 steps.
   8. Vehicles are initially distributed evenly with random speeds.

   Main parameters:

   | Parameter | Value |
   | --------- | ----- |
   | Maximum speed | 35 m/s (126 km/h) |
   | Acceleration | 2 m/s² |
   | Random deceleration | 3 m/s² |
   | Maximum deceleration | 5 m/s² |
   | HDV randomization probability | 0.2 |
   | HDV reaction time | 2 s |
   | CAV reaction time | 0.6 s |
   | CACC safety distance | 0.5 m |

   The authors investigate six CAV penetration rates:

   \[
   p=0\%,20\%,40\%,60\%,80\%,100\%
   \]

   **Note:** The paper reports a 400 m road but also describes it as 4000 cells of 0.01 m each, which is inconsistent. The plotted road positions extend to 40,000 cells, corresponding to 400 m.

5. Results: Influence of CAV penetration rate on road capacity.

   Figure 3 shows the velocity-density and flow-density relationships for different CAV penetration rates.

   1. As the CAV penetration rate increases:
      1. Average vehicle speed increases.
      2. Maximum traffic flow increases.
      3. Road capacity increases.
      4. Vehicles maintain free-flow conditions at higher densities.

   2. The authors find:
      1. At 0% CAV penetration, the road is entirely occupied by HDVs.
      2. At 60% CAV penetration, road capacity reaches approximately **1.97 times** the all-HDV capacity.
      3. At 80% CAV penetration, road capacity reaches approximately **3.24 times** the all-HDV capacity.
      4. At 100% CAV penetration, vehicles form stable platoons and travel at the maximum speed before density saturation.

   3. Under a pure CAV environment:
      1. Traffic flow increases approximately linearly with density.
      2. Average velocity remains at the maximum speed over the simulated density range.
      3. Vehicles do not experience random deceleration.
      4. One or more stable platoons form on the road.

   **Key result:** Increasing CAV penetration significantly improves road capacity, especially at high penetration rates. At 80% CAV penetration, capacity reaches approximately 3.24 times that of pure HDV traffic.

6. Results: Formation of CAV platoons.

   Figure 4 shows vehicle headway distributions at a traffic density of 60 vehicles/km.

   1. At low CAV penetration rates:
      1. Large platoons are difficult to form.
      2. Many CAVs follow HDVs and operate in ACC mode.
      3. Only small groups of consecutive CAVs can form platoons.

   2. As the CAV penetration rate increases:
      1. More consecutive CAVs appear on the road.

      4. Vehicle spacing within platoons decreases.

   3. At 100% CAV penetration:
      1. Every vehicle operates in CACC mode.
      2. All vehicles eventually belong to one or more platoons.
      3. Vehicles within each platoon maintain small, constant spacing.
      4. Platoons travel at the maximum speed.

   **Key result:** Higher CAV penetration increases the probability of platoon formation, allowing vehicles to maintain shorter headways and travel synchronously.

   1. At low penetration rates:
      1. Vehicle speeds fluctuate significantly.

   4. At 100% penetration, vehicles maintain nearly constant maximum speeds after platoon formation.

   **Key result:** High CAV penetration improves traffic stability by reducing random braking and synchronizing vehicle movement.

9. Results: Influence of CACC safety distance.

   Figure 8 investigates three CACC safety distances:

   \[
   d_s^{\mathrm{CACC}}=0.2,\ 0.5,\ 1.0\ \mathrm{m}
   \]
