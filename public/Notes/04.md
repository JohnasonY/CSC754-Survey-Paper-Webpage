# **Biham, O., Middleton, A. A., & Levine, D. (1992). Self-organization and a dynamical transition in traffic-flow models. Physical Review A, 46(10), R6124–R6127.**

1. Main idea: How can simple local vehicle movement rules produce large-scale traffic congestion and complete gridlock? The authors introduce a two-dimensional cellular automaton model, known as the Biham–Middleton–Levine (BML) model, to investigate how traffic systems transition from free-flowing movement to complete congestion.

2. Model I: Deterministic traffic flow.

   1. The road network is represented by an \(N \times N\) square lattice with periodic boundary conditions.
   2. Each cell can contain:
      1. One eastbound car (→)
      2. One northbound car (↑)
      3. No car
   3. Initially, cars are randomly distributed, with equal numbers traveling east and north.
   4. Cars follow these movement rules:
      1. At even time steps, only eastbound cars attempt to move one cell right.
      2. At odd time steps, only northbound cars attempt to move one cell up.
      3. A car moves only if its target cell is empty.
      4. Cars cannot occupy the same cell.
   5. The model is fully deterministic after initialization.

   Traffic density is defined as:

   \[
   p=\frac{\text{Total number of cars}}{N^2}
   \]

   Average velocity \(v\) is the fraction of attempted moves that succeed.

3. Low-density scenario: Free-flow phase.

   1. At sufficiently low density, cars organize into diagonal bands.
   2. Eastbound and northbound cars form patterns that avoid blocking one another.
   3. After the initial transient, all cars move successfully whenever their direction is updated.
   4. Authors find:
      1. Density: \(p=0.25\) in the illustrated simulation.
      2. Average velocity: **\(v=1\)**
      3. Traffic state: Completely free-flowing.

   **Key result:** Cars spontaneously organize into efficient traffic patterns without centralized coordination.

4. High-density scenario: Traffic gridlock.

   1. At high density, cars increasingly block one another.
   2. A large connected cluster forms diagonally across the lattice.
   3. The cluster prevents cars from moving in both directions.
   4. Eventually, the entire system becomes stationary.
   5. Authors find:
      1. Density: **\(p\approx0.4082\)**
      2. Average velocity: **\(v=0\)**
      3. Traffic state: Complete gridlock.

   **Key result:** Local interactions between cars can produce a system-wide traffic jam, even without accidents, complicated intersection rules, or external bottlenecks.

5. Critical density and phase transition.

   1. The authors simulate lattice sizes:
      1. \(16\times16\)
      2. \(32\times32\)
      3. \(64\times64\)
      4. \(128\times128\)
      5. \(512\times512\)

   2. The simulations show two distinct phases:
      1. Below the critical density \(p_c(N)\): \(v\approx1\)
      2. Above the critical density \(p_c(N)\): \(v\approx0\)

   3. As lattice size increases:
      1. The transition becomes sharper.
      2. The apparent critical density decreases.
      3. The range where flowing and jammed outcomes coexist becomes narrower.

   4. Approximate critical densities from Figure 3:

      | Lattice size | Critical density \(p_c(N)\), approximately |
      | ------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
      | 16 × 16      | 0.43                                        |
      | 32 × 32      | 0.38                                        |
      | 64 × 64      | 0.36                                        |
      | 128 × 128    | 0.34                                        |
      | 512 × 512    | 0.31                                        |

   **Key result:** The system exhibits a sharp, first-order-like transition from \(v=1\) to \(v=0\).

   The authors could not determine whether the critical density approaches a nonzero value or zero as the system size approaches infinity.

6. Equilibration time near the transition.

   1. Equilibration time is the number of simulation steps required for the system to reach a repeating configuration or become completely jammed.
   2. The authors find:
      1. Far from the transition, the system reaches its final state relatively quickly.
      2. Near the critical density, equilibration time increases dramatically.
      3. For the \(64\times64\) system, the peak exceeds \(10^6\) simulation steps.
      4. The peaks generally become sharper as system size increases, although the \(128\times128\) results are less clear.
   3. This makes estimating the critical density difficult.

   **Key result:** Traffic can remain in a long transient state near the critical density before eventually reaching free flow or complete gridlock.

7. Model II: Stochastic traffic flow.

   1. The alternating traffic-light rule is removed.
   2. Both eastbound and northbound cars attempt to move at every time step.
   3. When two cars attempt to enter the same empty cell:
      1. One is selected randomly.
      2. Each has a 50% probability of being selected.
   4. Authors find:
      1. A sharp jamming transition still occurs.
      2. For a \(512\times512\) lattice, the critical density is approximately **\(p_c\approx0.10\)**.
      3. The transition occurs at a lower density than in Model I.

   **Key result:** The sharp jamming transition is not dependent on deterministic traffic-light rules.

8. Comparison with one-dimensional traffic and Model III.

   1. The authors also study a one-dimensional traffic model on a periodic ring.
   2. In this model, cars move forward one cell whenever the next cell is empty.
   3. Its steady-state average velocity is analytically:

      \[
      v(p)=
      \begin{cases}
      1, & p\leq 0.5\\
      \dfrac{1-p}{p}, & p>0.5
      \end{cases}
      \]

   4. Unlike the two-dimensional model, velocity decreases continuously rather than abruptly.
   5. In Model III, the authors allow one eastbound and one northbound car to enter the same empty cell simultaneously.
   6. Model III also exhibits a continuous transition.

   **Key result:** The abrupt jamming transition arises from the competition between perpendicular traffic streams for the same road space.

9. Main conclusions.

   1. **Self-organization:** At low density, vehicles spontaneously form diagonal patterns that support maximum movement.

   2. **Complete gridlock:** At high density, a system-spanning blocking cluster emerges and stops all traffic.

   3. **Sharp phase transition:** Small changes in density near the critical point can cause a dramatic change in average velocity.

   4. **Finite-size effects:** Larger lattices exhibit sharper transitions at lower apparent critical densities.

   5. **Robustness:** Both deterministic and stochastic versions exhibit abrupt jamming.

   6. **Role of dimensionality:** Two-dimensional crossing traffic creates fundamentally different behavior from one-dimensional traffic.

   **Main contribution:** The paper demonstrates that simple local traffic rules can generate complex collective behavior, including spontaneous organization and abrupt system-wide gridlock.
