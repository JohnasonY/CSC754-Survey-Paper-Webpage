# **Rigler, A., & Turk, G. (2025). The influence of different driving parameters on traffic with a cellular automata model. Applied Sciences, 15(23), 12404.**

1. Main idea: How do acceleration behavior, driver reaction time, maximum acceleration, and speed limits affect traffic flow and congestion? The authors propose an improved cellular automaton model called the **Kinematic Theory (KT) model**, extending the LAI-E model with adaptive acceleration and heterogeneous driver reaction times.

2. Proposed KT model:

   1. Vehicles adjust their acceleration and deceleration rates according to available space and safety requirements.
   2. Unlike traditional CA models with fixed acceleration, vehicles can accelerate at a lower rate when there is insufficient space for maximum acceleration.
   3. The model considers different driver reaction times and acceleration capabilities.
   4. Safety distances are calculated considering vehicle speeds, reaction times, and emergency braking.

3. Simulation setup:

   1. Single-lane circular road with periodic boundary conditions.
   2. Road length: 1500 m (200 cells).
   3. Cell length: 7.5 m.
   4. Simulation duration: 10,000 time steps.
   5. Maximum speed: 60 km/h in the baseline scenario.
   6. Driver parameters:

      | Driver group | Proportion | Reaction time | Acceleration |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Fast reaction | 20% | 0.8 s | 4 m/s² |
      | Medium reaction | 60% | 1.0 s | 3 m/s² |
      | Slow reaction | 20% | 1.2 s | 2 m/s² |

4. Results:

   1. **Adaptive acceleration increases traffic flow.**
      - Compared with fixed acceleration, the KT model improves traffic flow by approximately **15–30% at medium densities** and more than **30% at high densities**.
      - At a maximum speed of 60 km/h and reaction time of 1 s, maximum flow reaches **2198 vehicles/h** at a density of **0.29 vehicles/cell**.
      - The model reproduces free flow, synchronized flow, and wide moving jams.

   2. **Shorter reaction times significantly improve capacity.**

      | Reaction time | Maximum flow (vehicles/h) |
      |---|---|
      | 0.8 s | 2565 |
      | 1.0 s | 2198 |
      | 1.2 s | 1952 |

      Reducing reaction time by 0.2 s can increase traffic flow by up to **40%** under congested conditions.

   3. **Higher speed limits increase maximum traffic flow.**
      - At 110 km/h, maximum flow reaches **2530 vehicles/h** at a density of **0.21 vehicles/cell**.
      - Maximum flow is approximately **23% higher** than at 50 km/h.
      - At high densities, speed limits have little influence because vehicles cannot reach their maximum speeds.

   4. **Maximum acceleration has limited influence.**
      - Differences in traffic flow between acceleration rates of 2, 3, and 4 m/s² are generally below 1%.
      - Available spacing and reaction time are more important than maximum acceleration capability.

   5. **Traffic phase transitions depend on density.**
      - At a reaction time of 1 s, free flow occurs below approximately 0.29 vehicles/cell.
      - Synchronized flow occurs around 0.29–0.31 vehicles/cell.
      - Congestion emerges around 0.32 vehicles/cell.
      - Flow decreases by approximately **230 vehicles/h** between densities of 0.32 and 0.33 vehicles/cell.

5. Main conclusions:

   1. Adaptive acceleration improves traffic capacity and delays congestion formation.
   2. Reaction time is one of the most influential parameters affecting traffic flow.
   3. Higher speed limits improve capacity primarily at low densities.
   4. Maximum acceleration capability has relatively little effect on steady-state traffic flow.
   5. The KT model reproduces three traffic phases using more realistic vehicle behavior, but its simulations are limited to single-lane roads.

   **Main contribution:** The paper demonstrates that introducing adaptive acceleration and heterogeneous reaction times into a cellular automaton model improves traffic-flow simulation and shows that reaction time and adaptive driving behavior have a greater influence on road capacity than maximum acceleration.
